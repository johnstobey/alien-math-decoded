Event Log

Running record from the Erdős disproof through the October 6 mass release.

May 2026
Erdős Unit Distance Conjecture Disproved
An internal OpenAI reasoning model disproved Paul Erdős's 1946 planar unit distance conjecture — a discrete geometry problem about how many times a unit distance can appear among n points. The model found the proof via algebraic number theory, class field towers, and Golod-Shafarevich theory. Princeton mathematician Will Sawin verified the construction's compounding behavior at scale. OpenAI did not design the system specifically for this problem; it used a general-purpose reasoning model. External mathematicians subsequently checked and verified the proof.
Verified Disproof
August 1, 2026
Ten Long-Open Problems — First Batch
OpenAI published ten results on problems open for at least a decade, each with machine-checkable Lean proofs and model reasoning narrations. Included: new upper bounds on sphere packing, a disproof of Connes's rigidity conjecture, and a construction of non-sofic groups. Chief scientist Jakub Pachocki described it as a "side-sprint." This was OpenAI's first structured release of AI-originated mathematical results, attributed explicitly to the AI rather than to human authors.
Lean Verified
September 8, 2026
Navier-Stokes Existence & Smoothness Announced
OpenAI announced a solution to the existence and smoothness problem for the Navier–Stokes equations — one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's seven Millennium Prize Problems ($1M each). The mathematical community had not yet verified the proof by the October 6 release. Mathematicians criticized OpenAI for announcing via blog post and social media rather than via a formal paper, making verification harder and leaving prior researchers' contributions uncredited.
Claimed — Not Yet Peer Reviewed
September 21, 2026
100+ Long-Standing Problems Resolved; Advisory Group Formed
OpenAI announced its internal model had resolved more than 100 long-standing open problems. Simultaneously, OpenAI announced an independent advisory group hosted at the Institute for Advanced Study (IAS). A group of 25 Fields Medalists — including Terence Tao and Peter Scholze — published a letter opposing the race by AI labs to solve major open problems without community coordination.
Unverified at Time
September 29, 2026
AGMAI Issues Guidelines — OpenAI Already in Violation
The Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence (AGMAI) published recommendations opening with a request that labs "stop testing advanced mathematical problems on proprietary models." OpenAI's own repository README states that testing on open research problems is "standard practice" in model development. OpenAI's October 6 release violated this guideline before it was a week old.
October 6, 2026
722 Manuscripts Published — github.com/openai/math
OpenAI published the repository openai/math on GitHub: 722 manuscripts in 372 result families across 17 mathematical disciplines, all produced by an unnamed internal frontier model. Supporting materials included PDF preprints, Lean formalizations for 162 manuscripts (22.4%), and 10 abridged reasoning summaries. Average compute per result: ~3 hours of ChatGPT Pro Thinking mode. Total estimated compute cost: ~$2,000 at Sol API rates. The model was posed approximately 4,000 open problems.
162 Lean-Verified 560 Claimed Only
October 7, 2026
Three Manuscripts Withdrawn; Catalogue Drops to 719
A sign error invalidated one argument; two dependent papers fell with it. OpenAI withdrew all three within 24 hours of publication, retaining prior versions per its announced correction policy. Mathematician Dana Moshkovitz (who worked for years toward the Unique Games Conjecture) reported that the claimed UGC proof was "so badly written she could not read it without AI help." OpenAI acknowledged that many results are not yet understood by its own mathematicians.
3 Withdrawn

Controversy Log

Mathematical Community Objections

  • AGMAI (advisory group hosted at IAS, which OpenAI itself convened) asked labs on September 29 to "stop testing advanced mathematical problems on proprietary models." OpenAI published 722 manuscripts eight days later.
  • 25 Fields Medalists including Terence Tao and Peter Scholze published a letter opposing the AI-lab race to solve open problems without community coordination (September 2026).
  • Mathematicians criticized OpenAI for announcing results — including Navier-Stokes — via blog posts and social media rather than papers, making verification harder and leaving prior researchers' contributions uncredited.
  • Dana Moshkovitz (years of work toward UGC) reported the claimed Unique Games proof was "so badly written she could not read it without AI help." OpenAI acknowledged many results are not yet understood by its own mathematicians.
  • OpenAI's earlier October 2025 claim that "GPT-5 solved 10 Erdős problems" was retracted after the model had simply retrieved known results from the internet. Kevin Weil's post was deleted.
  • Credit and attribution: open problems are how graduate students build careers. Mass AI-generated output preempts researchers mid-project without credit.
  • Only 162 of 722 manuscripts (22.4%) have Lean-verified main results. The remaining 560 are claims in ordinary prose that the community must read and referee.
What "Lean Verified" Means vs. "Peer Reviewed"
Lean Formalization ✓
A computer has checked that each logical step follows from its premises according to formal proof rules. This rules out a large class of errors — wrong implications, missing cases. It does not verify that the premises themselves are the right ones, that the theorem statement encodes the intended mathematical object, or that the argument is original rather than a retrieval of known results.
Peer Review
Domain experts read the argument in full, check that the theorem statement matches the intended problem, verify that the proof approach is sound, cross-check against existing literature, assess novelty and significance, and identify gaps a machine would not catch. None of the 722 manuscripts have completed peer review. Most have not yet been read in full by any human mathematician.
